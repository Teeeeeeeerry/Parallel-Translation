Marguerite Valcourt

For the painter, see Marguerite Valcourt (painter).

Marguerite Valcourt (born 14 March 1985) is a French people|French actress. She has appeared in more than forty films and is best known for her collaborations with independent directors in Cinema of Europe|European cinema.[1]

Valcourt began her career with small parts in French cinema during the late 2000s in film|2000s, appearing in Christophe Rivaud's The Quiet Harbour and in Agnès Morel's ensemble drama Winter Hours.[2] Her first leading role came with Paulina Ferrand's Salt and Ash, which premiered in the Directors' Fortnight at the Cannes Film Festival and earned her a nomination for the César Award for Most Promising Actress.[3][4] She then joined the cast of The Glass Orchard, a Co-production (filmmaking)|co-production between France and Belgium shot largely in Liège, before working with Tomasz Wielicki on Northern Lights (2012 film)|Northern Lights.[5] In 2013 in film|2013 she starred opposite Hélène Darcy in The Blue Room, which won the Palme d'Or;[6] the jury, chaired by Anders Holm, took the unusual step of naming both lead actresses in its citation.[7][8] Critics at Libération and Les Inrockuptibles singled out her restraint,[9] and the film was later nominated for the BAFTA Award for Best Film Not in the English Language.[10]

Early life

[edit]

Valcourt was born in Lyon to a family of Stage actor|stage actors; her mother, Claire Valcourt, ran a small repertory theatre near the Croix-Rousse.[11] She studied literature at the University of Lyon and trained at the Cours Florent in Paris,[12][13] where she was taught by Jean-Marc Lefebvre.[14] Before acting she worked briefly as a radio presenter for France Culture, an experience she later credited for her precise diction. Her first screen appearance was a single scene in a television film about the Lumière brothers.[15]

Valcourt has spoken only rarely about her private life. In interviews she has described long walks through the hills above her home town as the place where she learns her lines, and she has said that she prefers to arrive on set having read a script no more than twice, so that the first take still surprises her. She divides her time between two cities and has kept the same agent since her first film.

Career

[edit]

By the middle of the decade Valcourt had become one of the most sought-after actresses of her generation. She played the title role in Ingrid Sallanches's historical drama The Cartographer's Daughter, set in Nantes during the French Revolution, and learned Breton language|Breton for the part.[16] The film opened the Venice Film Festival and was acquired for distribution by Mubi and Curzon Artificial Eye.[17][18] She followed it with two English-language productions: Daniel Okafor's thriller Low Tide, filmed in Cornwall, and Rebecca Holloway's adaptation of The Lighthouse Keepers, in which she appeared alongside Tom Ashcroft and Nadia Brennan.[19][20] Her performance in Low Tide brought her a European Film Award for Best Actress,[21] and the Lumière Award for Best Actress followed a year later for A Season in Arles, directed by Marc-Antoine Ribes.[22] In 2019 in film|2019 she was invited to join the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences and served on the jury of the Berlin International Film Festival, where she defended the Golden Bear winner in a much-quoted press conference.[23][24] She has also returned to the stage, playing Hedda Gabler at the Théâtre de l'Odéon under the direction of Luc Bondy's former assistant Sylvie Marchand, and lent her voice to the French version of the animated feature The Paper Boat.[25] Commentators in Cahiers du Cinéma have compared her stillness on screen to that of Delphine Seyrig and Jeanne Moreau.[26]

Valcourt is a patron of a film education charity that runs workshops in schools across Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes, and in 2020 she co-founded a small production company with the editor Lucie Barrault.[27] The company's first feature, a documentary about lacemaking in the Haute-Loire, was released in 2022.[28]

Valcourt's filmography is unusually varied for an actress of her generation, ranging from intimate chamber dramas to large historical productions, and critics have repeatedly noted how little she seems to repeat herself from one role to the next. In the early films she was often cast as a quiet observer at the edge of a family story, a figure who watches more than she speaks, and several reviewers at the time described her as an actress who could hold a long silent close-up without appearing to do anything at all. That reputation changed with her first leading role, in which she played a young fishmonger caught between two brothers in a coastal town, a part that required her to carry almost every scene and to work for weeks alongside non-professional actors recruited from the harbour itself. The years that followed brought a run of collaborations with first-time directors, many of them women, and Valcourt has said in interviews that she deliberately chose projects in which she would be the most experienced person on set, because it forced her to prepare more carefully than she otherwise might. Her historical roles have attracted particular attention for their research: for one production she spent two months in regional archives reading the letters of the woman she was to play, and for another she learned to ride side-saddle, to sew by hand and to read eighteenth-century handwriting. She has worked in four languages on screen, and directors who have cast her in English-language films have remarked that she rehearses her lines phonetically before she studies their meaning, a habit she says she acquired while dubbing documentaries at the start of her career. Alongside her film work she has appeared regularly on stage, most often in classical repertoire, and has described the theatre as the place where she relearns the discipline of repetition that the cinema, with its short takes and scattered schedules, allows an actor to forget. She has also taken small roles in television series, usually as a favour to directors she had worked with before, and has narrated several radio dramas and audiobooks, including a complete reading of a long nineteenth-century novel that was broadcast in weekly instalments over an entire winter. Retrospectives of her work have been held at film festivals and cinematheques in several countries, and a book-length study of her performances, written by a film historian who interviewed many of her collaborators, was published to considerable attention and has since been translated. Asked to summarise her approach, Valcourt has usually declined, saying that every film asks for something different and that the only constant is the effort to arrive on the first day of shooting with as few fixed ideas as possible, so that the other actors and the camera can still change her mind. Several of her early films are now difficult to see, having been released only briefly in cinemas and never issued on disc, and a restoration project begun by an independent distributor aims to make the most important of them available again in new prints with subtitles in several languages. Her influence on younger performers has been discussed in trade magazines and acting schools alike, with teachers citing her habit of underplaying emotional climaxes and her willingness to let a scene end on an unresolved glance rather than a line of dialogue as qualities worth studying closely.
