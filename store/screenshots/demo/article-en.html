Deep Sea

Life Without Sunlight

Two kilometres below the surface, entire ecosystems run on chemistry instead of light. What they eat, and how we found them.

Long read · 11 minutes

For most of the history of biology, one assumption went unquestioned: everything alive is ultimately fed by the sun. Plants capture sunlight, animals eat plants, and the whole structure rests on that single foundation. Textbooks drew the arrow the same way for a century, and nobody had much reason to redraw it.

That assumption broke in 1977, on the floor of the Pacific Ocean. A research submersible descended along a volcanic ridge near the Galápagos, expecting bare rock and near-freezing water. Instead the crew found shimmering plumes of hot fluid, and clustered around them, animals in improbable density: white crabs, clams the size of dinner plates, and tube worms taller than a person.

Chemistry as a food supply

The fluid venting from those cracks carries dissolved hydrogen sulfide, drawn out of the rock by heat from the magma below. To most animals the compound is lethal. To certain bacteria it is dinner. They strip electrons from the sulfide and use the released energy to build sugars from carbon dioxide, a process that needs no light at all.

Everything else at the vent is built on those bacteria. Some grazers scrape them off rock surfaces. Others took a stranger path. The giant tube worm has no mouth, no gut, and no anus. What it has instead is an organ packed with billions of symbiotic bacteria, which the worm supplies with sulfide and oxygen carried by a specialised form of haemoglobin. The bacteria feed the worm from the inside.

The discovery did not add a new branch to the tree of life. It added a second root.

Vent communities are also short-lived by geological standards. A vent may run for a few decades before the plumbing beneath it seals or shifts, and when the flow stops the community starves within months. Every population is a temporary tenant on a landscape that keeps rewriting itself.

What the vents changed

Four decades of follow-up work turned a curiosity into a set of arguments that reach well beyond marine biology:

  • Sunlight is one energy source for life, not the only one — chemical gradients work just as well
  • The temperature ceiling for life moved upward, repeatedly, as new vent microbes were cultured
  • Symbiosis looks less like an exception and more like a standard engineering solution
  • Moons with subsurface oceans and active interiors became serious targets in the search for life

That last point is why planetary scientists still cite a Pacific dive from the 1970s. Europa and Enceladus both appear to hold liquid water over rocky interiors, and Enceladus vents plumes into space that instruments have flown through. If life needs only water, rock, and a chemical gradient, then the list of plausible addresses in the solar system grows considerably longer.

An unfinished map

Most of the mid-ocean ridge system — some sixty thousand kilometres of it — has never been surveyed at the resolution needed to spot an active vent. Each detailed expedition tends to return with species new to science, which suggests the catalogue is nowhere near complete.

The urgency is not purely academic. The same deposits that make vents biologically remarkable also make them commercially interesting, and mining proposals now arrive faster than the surveys that would tell us what is there. Deciding what to protect requires first knowing what exists, and on that question the ocean floor is still mostly a blank sheet.
